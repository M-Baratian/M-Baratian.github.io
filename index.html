About

I am a Ph.D. candidate in economics at Tulane University, working in health economics, applied microeconomics, and the economics of innovation. Much of my work asks how public programs and large shocks change behavior in ways that only surface years later.

My job market paper uses COVID-19 to trace how a global shock redirects scientific research effort across fields, pairing an NLP measurement of roughly four million PubMed papers with event-study methods. A separate strand studies the U.S. safety net: how the 1990s shift to “work-first” welfare shaped children into adulthood, and how the generosity of SNAP benefits supports mental health when households are under strain.

I lean on quasi-experimental designs and machine learning, and I care about measurement as much as identification.

Research

The Shadow Price of Parental Time: Long-Run Human Capital Effects of the “Work-First” Welfare Transition

Job Market Paper

Draft available on request

The 1990s shift from entitlement-based cash assistance to “work-first” welfare pulled millions of low-income mothers into employment, changing the time environment of early childhood for a generation of children. I ask how exposure to this transition between conception and age five affects those children’s outcomes in adulthood.

Read full abstract

Using the Panel Study of Income Dynamics and children’s state of birth, I measure each child’s exposure as the share of a 69-month window spent under AFDC waivers (1992–1996) or TANF (1996–1997), and I estimate its long-run effect with a staggered difference-in-differences design in the spirit of Hoynes, Schanzenbach, and Almond (2016). Adults who spent more of early childhood under the reform are substantially more likely to be employed, a result that holds under stacked difference-in-differences, the Callaway–Sant’Anna estimator, wild cluster bootstrap inference, and Romano–Wolf correction for multiple testing. What moves the outcome is the duration of exposure rather than the harshness of a state’s rules: sanction severity, time limits, and work-requirement stringency show no gradient. The employment gain does not extend to health, where the estimates are sensitive to the estimator and lean negative. These patterns are consistent with a reform that raised the shadow price of parental time at home, trading gains in later labor-market attachment against investment in early childhood.

Shocks, Reallocation, and the Direction of Innovation: The Case of COVID-19

Working Paper

Working paper · SSRN 6871958

Shocks reallocate attention, lab capacity, and funding across fields. I ask how major shocks reshape the field composition of research effort and whether substitution or complementarity dominates. Using COVID-19 as a global shock, I map PubMed articles (2017–2022) into 30 topical clusters with NLP and construct monthly research-effort indices, then separate level shifts from trend breaks using interrupted time series and a stacked event-study difference-in-differences.

Read full abstract

Three patterns emerge. First, trend slowdowns: relative to pre-period growth, the median cluster’s monthly growth rate falls by about seven percentage points, with many clusters registering statistically significant declines. Second, substitution toward the shock-proximate area: negatively exposed fields experience within-cluster effort drops on the order of 10–13% relative to their counterfactual pre-trends, while the COVID-19 cluster’s share of total effort rises sharply at the peak. Third, complementarities: a subset of method- or platform-adjacent clusters accelerates by roughly 4–6 percentage points. These findings imply that policymakers should prioritize neglected clusters during shocks—via bridge funding and access safeguards—to mitigate unintended crowd-out, while supporting complementary platforms that scale beneficial spillovers. Beyond COVID-19, the measurement and identification strategy provides a general framework to characterize the structure of reallocation under any major shock.

SNAP Generosity and Mental Health

Working Paper
