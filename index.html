About

I am a Ph.D. candidate in economics at Tulane University, working in health economics, applied microeconomics, and the economics of innovation. Much of my work asks how public programs and large shocks change behavior in ways that only surface years later.

I am drawn to questions where the key variable has never been measured. The part I enjoy most is pulling evidence out of unstructured data, the text and records that never arrive as tidy tables, and turning it into measures I can use to study welfare programs and the health and economic outcomes they shape.

My job market paper studies the U.S. safety net. Using the Panel Study of Income Dynamics, I ask how the 1990s shift to “work-first” welfare changed the time environment of early childhood for a generation of children, and how that exposure surfaces in their adult outcomes decades later. Alongside it, one project asks how the generosity of SNAP benefits supports mental health when households are under strain, and another uses COVID-19 to trace how a large shock redirects scientific research effort across fields, pairing an NLP measurement of roughly four million PubMed papers with event-study methods.

I lean on quasi-experimental designs and machine learning, and I care about measurement as much as identification.

Research

The Children of Welfare Reform: Early Childhood Under the New Rules and Adult Outcomes

Job Market Paper

Draft available on request

Welfare reform in the 1990s replaced Aid to Families with Dependent Children (AFDC), a cash entitlement for poor families, with Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF), aid conditioned on work and limited to a fixed number of months. Those rules raised parents’ employment but cut cash income and the time parents had with their children, so whether the young children in those families gained or lost is an open question. This paper follows those children into adulthood and asks whether more time under stricter rules in early childhood changed their general health, mental health and employment outcomes.

Read full abstract

Because the rules differed across states, this paper scores each state’s rules year by year, from 0 under the AFDC rules to 1 at the strictest setting any state used. A child’s strictness-adjusted years of exposure are the months from conception to age five spent under reform, weighted by the state’s score. Those years should matter most where the family was likely to receive welfare. The paper therefore compares children of the same birth state and cohort whose families had higher and lower predicted probabilities of welfare receipt. The comparison is made in the Panel Study of Income Dynamics for children born between 1975 and 1999. Each strictness-adjusted year of exposure is associated with a 0.05 standard deviation higher general health index (self-rated health and obesity) and a 0.07 standard deviation higher mental health index (psychological distress and psychiatric diagnosis) when a child whose family was likely to be on welfare is compared with one whose family was not. Both estimates are significant at the 10 percent level. Estimates that count the months under reform without scoring their strictness are smaller, so part of the effect comes from what the rules required and not only from how long they applied. The same exposure is not associated with a change in the children’s employment. The estimates for their hours and earnings are imprecise. Taken together, these results suggest that stricter rules in early childhood left the children the reform reached healthier than their peers on both health indices.

Shocks, Reallocation, and the Direction of Innovation: The Case of COVID-19

Working Paper

Working paper · draft available on request

When a shock raises the return to one line of research, researchers can change fields or absorb the new questions into the work they already do. This paper measures which margin carried the adjustment of biomedical research to COVID-19. A classifier built with BioBERT, a biomedical language model, assigns every PubMed publication from 2018 through 2022, an estimation sample of about four million papers, a probability of belonging to each of thirty-one research fields. Averaging these probabilities within a quarter gives each field’s share of attention, and I estimate the change in each share at the first quarter of 2020.

Read full abstract

Every one of the thirty-one field shares broke at the onset, and all survive a false discovery correction. The COVID field absorbed about three percentage points of total attention. Sixteen fields gained and fifteen lost, and the pattern did not follow proximity to coronavirus science. The adjustment ran within the fields instead. Researchers raised the coronavirus content of papers inside their home fields by 3.2 percentage points, and by 5.8 when papers are weighted by journal quality, and the rise appears at every tier of venue quality. This combination is what a model predicts when changing fields is costly and blending shock content into ongoing work is cheap. For a funder trying to redirect science in a crisis, the responsive margin is within fields rather than between them.
